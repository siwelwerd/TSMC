    Definition 1.7.2.

    Let \(f(x)\) be \(n\)-times differentiable at \(x=c\text{.}\) The Taylor polynomial of degree \(n\) at \(x=c\) is the polynomial \(\displaystyle T_n(x)=\sum _{k=1} ^n \dfrac{f^{(k)}(c)(x-c)^k}{k!|}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Observation 1.7.3.

    By construction, if \(T_n(x)\) is the degree \(n\) Taylor polynomial for \(f(x)\) at \(x=c\text{,}\) then \(T_n ^{(i)}(c)=f^{(i)}(c)\) for all \(0 \leq i \leq n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.7.4.

    A power series is a function of the form
    \begin{equation*} P(x)=\sum _{n=0} ^\infty a_n (x-c)^n \end{equation*}
    for some real numbers \(c\) and \(a_n\text{.}\) That is, \(P(x)\) is defined to be the value that the series converges to where it converges, and is undefined elsewhere.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 1.7.8.

    Let \(\displaystyle \sum _{n=0} ^\infty a_n (x-c)^n\) be a power series. Then either
    • The series converges only at \(x=c\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The series converges everywhere
      🔗
      🔗
    • There exists \(R\) such that the series converges absolutely on the open interval \((c-R,c+R)\) and diverges on \((-\infty, c-R) \cup (c+R,\infty)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    This follows from Lemma 1.7.10 below. Let
    \begin{equation*} L=\sup \left\{ x\ \mid\ \sum _{n=0} ^\infty a_n (x-c)^n \text{ converges} \right\}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Note that \(L \geq c\text{.}\) If \(L=c\) the series converges only at \(c\) (). If \(L=\infty\text{,}\) then Lemma 1.7.10 guarantees convergence everywhere. Finally, assume \(L=c+R \gt c\text{.}\) Then by Lemma 1.7.10 the series converges absolutely on the interval \((c-R,c+R)\text{.}\) Moreover, by construction if the series converges at some point \(b \gt c+R\text{,}\) then \(b \leq L=c+R\text{,}\) a contradiction; and similarly, if it converges at some point \(b \lt c-R\text{,}\) then by Lemma 1.7.10 it converges at a point larger than \(c+R\text{,}\) producing the same contradiction. So the series must diverge on \((-\infty,c-R) \cup (c+R,\infty)\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.7.9.

    For a power series \(\displaystyle \sum _{n=0} ^\infty a_n (x-c)^n\text{,}\) the maximum interval on which it converges is called the interval of convergence. Since by Theorem 1.7.8 this interval is symmetric about \(c\) of length \(2R\text{,}\) \(R\) is called the radius of convergence.
    🔗
    🔗

    Lemma 1.7.10.

    Let \(\displaystyle \sum _{n=0} ^\infty a_n (x-c)^n\) be a power series. If the series converges at \(x=r\text{,}\) then it converges absolutely on the interval \(|x-c| \lt |r-c|\text{,}\) i.e. at all points closer to \(c\) than \(r\) is.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    By making a substitution \(y=x-c\text{,}\) we may assume without loss of generality that \(c=0\text{.}\) Assume further without loss of generality that \(r \gt 0\text{,}\) and let \(-r \lt b \lt r\) be given. By the ratio test, since \(\sum _{n=0} ^\infty a_n r^n\) converges, we must have \(|a_nr^n|^\frac{1}{n} \leq 1\) (since if bigger than 1, the series would diverge by the ratio test). Then we compute
    \begin{equation*} |a_nb^n|^\frac{1}{n}=b|a_n|^\frac{1}{n} \lt |r||a_n|^\frac{1}{n} \leq 1 \end{equation*}
    Thus we see by the ratio test that \(\displaystyle \sum _{n=1} ^\infty a_n b^n\) converges absolutely.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.7.11.

    Note that Theorem 1.7.8 provides no information about convergence at the endpoints of the interval of convergence. These must each be checked individually.

    Theorem 1.7.13.

    Let \(\displaystyle f(x)=\sum _{n=0} ^\infty a_n (x-c)^n \) be a power series that converges on \((c-R,c+R)\) for some \(R\text{.}\) Then
    • \(\displaystyle f^\prime(x)=\sum _{n=1} ^\infty na_n(x-c)^{n-1}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \int f(x)\ dx=\sum _{n=0} ^\infty \frac{a_n}{n+1}(x-c)^{n+1}+C\)
      🔗
      🔗
    and these series both also converge on \((c-R,c+R)\text{.}\)
