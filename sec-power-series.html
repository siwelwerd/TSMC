    Section 1.7 Power Series

    Activity 1.7.1.

    Consider the function \(f(x)=e^x\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Find a linear approximation \(L_1(x)\) to \(f(x)\) at \(x=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    \(L_1(x)\) should satisfy \(L_1(0)=f(0)\) and \(L_1^\prime(0)=f^\prime(0)\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(L_1(x)=1+x\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find a quadratic approximation \(L_2(x)\) to \(f(x)\) at \(x=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    \(L_2(x)\) should satisfy
    • \(\displaystyle L_2(0)=f(0)\)
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle L_2^\prime(0)=f^\prime(0)\)
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle L_2^{\prime \prime}(0)=f^{\prime \prime}(0)\)
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(L_2(x)=1+x+\frac{1}{2}x^2\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Find a degree 3 approximation \(L_3(x)\) to \(f(x)\) at \(x=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    \(L_3(x)\) should satisfy
    • \(\displaystyle L_3(0)=f(0)\)
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle L_3^\prime(0)=f^\prime(0)\)
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle L_3^{\prime \prime}(0)=f^{\prime \prime}(0)\)
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle L_3^{\prime \prime \prime }(0)=f^{\prime \prime \prime}(0)\)
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(L_3(x)=1+x+\frac{1}{2}x^2+\frac{1}{6}x^3\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Evaluate your three approximations at \(x=0.1\text{.}\) How do they compare to \(e^{0.1}\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Find a degree \(n\) approximation \(L_n(x)\) to \(f(x)\) at \(x=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\displaystyle L_n(x)=\sum _{k=0} ^n \frac{x^k}{k!}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    Plot your degree \(n\) approximation in Desmos alongside the function \(f(x)=e^x\text{.}\) Add a slider and observe what happens as \(n\) increases?

    Definition 1.7.2.

    Let \(f(x)\) be \(n\)-times differentiable at \(x=c\text{.}\) The Taylor polynomial of degree \(n\) at \(x=c\) is the polynomial \(\displaystyle T_n(x)=\sum _{k=0} ^n \dfrac{f^{(k)}(c)(x-c)^k}{k!}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Observation 1.7.3.

    By construction, if \(T_n(x)\) is the degree \(n\) Taylor polynomial for \(f(x)\) at \(x=c\text{,}\) then \(T_n ^{(i)}(c)=f^{(i)}(c)\) for all \(0 \leq i \leq n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.7.4.

    A power series is a function of the form
    \begin{equation*} P(x)=\sum _{n=0} ^\infty a_n (x-c)^n \end{equation*}
    for some real numbers \(c\) and \(a_n\text{.}\) That is, \(P(x)\) is defined to be the value that the series converges to where it converges, and is undefined elsewhere.
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 1.7.5.

    Show that the power series \(\displaystyle \sum _{n=0} ^\infty \frac{x^n}{n!}\) converges everywhere, i.e. for every value of \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We apply the ratio test. Compute
    \begin{equation*} \lim _{n\rightarrow \infty} \left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right| = \frac{n!}{(n+1)!}\left|\frac{x^{n+1}}{x^n}\right| = |x| \lim _{n \rightarrow \infty} \frac{1}{n+1}=0 \end{equation*}
    Since this limit is \(0 \lt 1\) for all values of \(x\text{,}\) this series converges for all \(x\) (i.e. it converges everywhere).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 1.7.6.

    Determine where the power series \(\displaystyle \sum _{n=0} ^\infty (-1)^nx^n\) converges.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We apply the ratio test. Compute
    \begin{equation*} \lim _{n\rightarrow \infty} \left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right| = |x| \end{equation*}
    Thus by the ratio test we know the series converges when \(|x|\lt 1\) and diverges when \(|x| \gt 1\text{.}\) Since the series \(\sum _{n=0} ^\infty (-1)^n(1)^n\) diverges, and the series \(\sum _{n=0} ^\infty (-1)^n(-1)^n\) also diverges, the power series converges precisely on the interval \((-1,1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 1.7.7.

    Determine where the power series \(\displaystyle \sum _{n=1} ^\infty 3^n(x-1)^n\) converges.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We apply the ratio test. Compute
    \begin{equation*} \lim _{n\rightarrow \infty} \left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right| = \lim _{n \rightarrow \infty} \frac{3^{n+1}}{3^n} \frac{|x-1|^{n+1}}{|x-1|^n}=3|x-1| \end{equation*}
    Thus by the ratio test we know the series converges when \(3|x-1|\lt 1\text{,}\) i.e. on \(\left(\frac{2}{3},\frac{4}{3}\right)\) and diverges when \(3|x-1| \gt 1\text{.}\) Since the series \(\sum _{n=0} ^\infty (3)^n(\frac{4}{3}-1)^n\) diverges, and the series \(\sum _{n=0} ^\infty (3)^n(\frac{2}{3}-1)^n\) also diverges, the power series converges precisely on the interval \(\left(\frac{2}{3}, \frac{4}{3}\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 1.7.8.

    Let \(\displaystyle \sum _{n=0} ^\infty a_n (x-c)^n\) be a power series. Then either
    • The series converges only at \(x=c\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The series converges everywhere
      🔗
      🔗
    • There exists \(R\) such that the series converges absolutely on the open interval \((c-R,c+R)\) and diverges on \((-\infty, c-R) \cup (c+R,\infty)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    This follows from Lemma 1.7.10 below. Let
    \begin{equation*} L=\sup \left\{ x\ \middle | \ \sum _{n=0} ^\infty a_n (x-c)^n \text{ converges} \right\}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Note that \(L \geq c\text{.}\) If \(L=c\) the series converges only at \(c\text{.}\) If \(L=\infty\text{,}\) then Lemma 1.7.10 guarantees convergence everywhere. Finally, assume \(L=c+R \gt c\text{.}\) Then by Lemma 1.7.10 the series converges absolutely on the interval \((c-R,c+R)\text{.}\) Moreover, by construction if the series converges at some point \(b \gt c+R\text{,}\) then \(b \leq L=c+R\text{,}\) a contradiction; and similarly, if it converges at some point \(b \lt c-R\text{,}\) then by Lemma 1.7.10 it converges at a point larger than \(c+R\text{,}\) producing the same contradiction. So the series must diverge on \((-\infty,c-R) \cup (c+R,\infty)\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.7.9.

    For a power series \(\displaystyle \sum _{n=0} ^\infty a_n (x-c)^n\text{,}\) the maximum interval on which it converges is called the interval of convergence. Since by Theorem 1.7.8 this interval is symmetric about \(c\) of length \(2R\text{,}\) \(R\) is called the radius of convergence.
    🔗
    🔗

    Lemma 1.7.10.

    Let \(\displaystyle \sum _{n=0} ^\infty a_n (x-c)^n\) be a power series. If the series converges at \(x=r\text{,}\) then it converges absolutely on the interval \(|x-c| \lt |r-c|\text{,}\) i.e. at all points closer to \(c\) than \(r\) is.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    By making a substitution \(y=x-c\text{,}\) we may assume without loss of generality that \(c=0\text{.}\) Assume further without loss of generality that \(r \gt 0\text{,}\) and let \(-r \lt b \lt r\) be given. By the root test, since \(\displaystyle \sum _{n=0} ^\infty a_n r^n\) converges, we must have \(|a_nr^n|^\frac{1}{n} \leq 1\) (since if bigger than 1, the series would diverge by the root test). Then we compute
    \begin{equation*} |a_nb^n|^\frac{1}{n}=b|a_n|^\frac{1}{n} \lt |r||a_n|^\frac{1}{n} \leq 1 \end{equation*}
    Thus we see by the root test that \(\displaystyle \sum _{n=1} ^\infty a_n b^n\) converges absolutely.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.7.11.

    Note that Theorem 1.7.8 provides no information about convergence at the endpoints of the interval of convergence. These must each be checked individually.
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 1.7.12.

    Find the interval of convergence for each of the following power series.
    🔗

    (a)

    \(\displaystyle \sum _{n=1} ^\infty \frac{x^n}{3n}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Using the ratio test,
    \begin{equation*} \lim _{n \rightarrow \infty} \left| \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right| = \lim _{n \rightarrow \infty} |x| \frac{n+1}{n}=|x| \end{equation*}
    so the series converges when \(|x| \lt 1\text{.}\) At the right endpoint the series simplifies to a harmonic series, while at the left endpoint it simplifies to an alternating harmonic series, so the interval of convergence is \([-1,1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    \(\displaystyle \sum _{n=1} ^\infty \frac{(x-2)^n}{\sqrt{n}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Applying the ratio test, we see that the series converges on \(|x-2| \lt 1\text{.}\) At \(x=3\) we get a divergent \(p\)-series, while the alternating series test provides convergence at \(x=1\text{,}\) so the interval of convergence is \([1,3)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    \(\displaystyle \sum _{n=2} ^\infty \frac{(x+3)^n}{\ln(n)}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \([-4,-2)\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 1.7.13.

    Let \(\displaystyle f(x)=\sum _{n=0} ^\infty a_n (x-c)^n \) be a power series that converges on \((c-R,c+R)\) for some \(R\text{.}\) Then
    • \(\displaystyle f^\prime(x)=\sum _{n=1} ^\infty na_n(x-c)^{n-1}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \int f(x)\ dx=\sum _{n=0} ^\infty \frac{a_n}{n+1}(x-c)^{n+1}+C\)
      🔗
      🔗
    and these series both also converge on \((c-R,c+R)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 1.7.14.

    Let \(\displaystyle f(x)=\sum _{n=0} ^\infty \dfrac{x^n}{n!}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Compute \(f^\prime(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(f^\prime(x)=f(x)\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Compute \(\displaystyle \int f(x)\ dx\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\displaystyle \int f(x)\ dx= f(x)+C\)
